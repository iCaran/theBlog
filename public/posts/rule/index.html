I. A Puzzle of Survival

Every other major polytheism the ancient world produced is now, in the fullest sense, gone. No one prays to Amun-Ra expecting the Nile to flood on schedule. No one leaves offerings for Odin before a crossing. The gods of Athens and Rome survive only as literature, marble, and metaphor. In each case, a monotheistic tradition arrived, organized itself, and displaced what came before, not through gradual attrition but through something closer to a systematic institutional campaign. Only one comparably ancient tradition has resisted that pattern to the point of remaining a living, majority faith in its home territory: the loosely bundled complex of practice, text, and community that gets called, for lack of a more precise word, Hinduism.

This is worth pausing on, because on paper it should have been the easiest target. It has no central authority, no single creed, no unified clergy, no missionary infrastructure of its own, and until recently, little capacity for the kind of coordinated response organized religions typically mount against rivals. And yet here it is, several millennia and several determined waves of proselytizing later, still the majority tradition of well over a billion people. The interesting question isn’t whether that survival happened. It’s what actually did the work of holding it together, given that the thing which usually performs that function in surviving religions, institutional structure, was largely missing.

II. Two Models of Organization

Put a parish next to a neighborhood mandir and the difference in kind, not degree, becomes obvious. A parish sits inside a chain of command that terminates, eventually, in a central authority; it receives doctrine and personnel from above and reports back up the same line. Something broadly similar has historically been true of the mosque, which functions as a place of worship and a coordination point at once, where community decisions get made and disputes get referred upward. The mandir does neither. It answers to no diocese. There is no office whose approval a new temple needs, no bishop with jurisdiction over a district of them. The closest thing most Indian temples experience to oversight is secular: state temple boards concerned with taxation and trust administration, not doctrine.

The consequence is real. The social and administrative functions bundled into a church or mosque elsewhere, mutual aid, dispute resolution, communal decision-making, lived somewhere else entirely in Hindu society. The village panchayat did that work, and the panchayat was never a religious body. Spiritual authority and worldly coordination stayed separated in a way that has no clean equivalent in the Abrahamic traditions. Whatever gave Hindu society its cohesion, it plainly wasn’t this.

III. The Economics of Belonging

There’s a useful, slightly deflationary idea in the sociology of religion: that a tradition’s ability to hold and gain adherents often has less to do with the persuasiveness of its theology than with the tangible infrastructure built around it. A convert doesn’t only acquire new beliefs. In a well-organized tradition, they acquire a network, people who help them find work, arrange a marriage, lend money in an emergency, see to their burial. Belief is the stated reason. Belonging, in the thick and practical sense, is very often the actual mechanism, which is part of why organized religion has historically done best among the economically precarious. It isn’t only selling metaphysics. It’s selling security, and security has always closed better than doctrine.

If that’s right, an “unorganized” religion has an obvious structural problem. Without a central body running the equivalent of hospitals, schools, and mutual aid funds under one banner, what keeps people inside the fold once a better-resourced alternative offers exactly that kind of security instead? For most of the last two centuries, the honest institutional answer has been: not much. And yet the fold has, broadly, held. Something else must have been doing that job.

IV. Varna as Substrate

Here is a hypothesis worth sitting with, even though it cuts against how caste is usually discussed. What if the incentive structure that organized religions build on purpose, Hindu society had already built, more or less by accident, out of something else entirely: the jati and varna system.

Strip away, for a moment, the theology layered onto caste over time, and look only at its economic function. A jati was, among other things, an occupational guild: a network that transmitted a trade, guaranteed a customer base within the local economy, arranged marriages inside a known and trusted pool, and offered mutual obligation in hard times. To leave the religious fold, on this reading, was never merely to change a metaphysical commitment. It meant becoming “varna-less,” severing the guild membership that secured one’s livelihood, marriage prospects, and standing in the local economy. Conversion carried a price denominated in this life, not the next, and the price was steep. If that holds even partially, the varna system, not any central doctrine or clergy, may have been the real load-bearing structure. Some go further and argue that varna is the one genuinely consistent thread running through Hindu practice’s enormous internal variation, functionally closer to “the religion” than the religion itself.

This is a functionalist reading, and it should be held as exactly that: one lens developed to explain a pattern, not a verdict on caste as it was lived or as it persists into eras where it has caused, and continues to cause, serious and well-documented harm. A structure can be doing real organizational work and be, independently, unjust. Both are true at once, and the second doesn’t disprove the first.

V. Reform Without Rupture

The claim gets odd support from India’s long history of internal religious reform. Buddhism, Jainism, and much later Sikhism each arose partly as a response to exactly the incoherence described above, each offering a cleaner, more explicit identity than the sprawling, undefined mass of Vedic practice surrounding it. Modern movements like ISKCON can be read as a smaller-scale version of the same instinct: a distilled, tightly bounded identity carved out of a vaguer surrounding tradition.

What’s notable is what none of these movements did. Not one of them, across more than two thousand years, positioned itself as the enemy of the underlying tradition it was reforming or departing from. Each maintained something like formal deference even while building something distinct. Compare this to how the Abrahamic traditions eventually related to the polytheisms of Egypt, Scandinavia, Greece, and Rome, a relationship that ended in displacement, not coexistence. India generated dissent constantly. It essentially never generated dissent willing to declare the core tradition an enemy to be defeated rather than a parent to be modified. Whatever kept that line from being crossed functioned as an unwritten rule, and an unwritten rule that holds for two millennia across wildly different reform movements is not an accident. It’s evidence of some underlying structural logic, even where nobody ever wrote the logic down.

VI. The Trouble with Definition

All of this runs into a genuinely hard problem the moment you try to state, plainly, who counts as a Hindu. The tradition has no baptism, no single act of affiliation. Vinayak Savarkar’s early twentieth-century attempt defined the category territorially and culturally rather than theologically: someone who regards India as both fatherland and holy land. Others insist on a text-based standard instead, treating acceptance of Vedic authority as the real dividing line, which immediately raises the question of what to do with the nastik traditions that reject that authority while still developing, historically, within the same civilizational space. Still others treat the category as maximally elastic: Hindu as anyone who doesn’t actively claim another religious identity, which produces the odd result that agnosticism, atheism, and outright rejection of every scriptural claim can all sit comfortably under the same label.

None of these answers is obviously wrong, which is precisely the difficulty. An identity this elastic is, on one hand, a genuine strength. It has let enormous internal diversity coexist without the kind of intergroup conflict a harder-edged boundary tends to produce, since sects that never had to compete for one orthodox seat never developed the zero-sum incentives that produce holy wars. On the other hand, an identity with no clear non-negotiables has a much harder time recognizing an external threat as a threat, or mounting any collective response to one, because there is no agreed floor beneath which someone has clearly left the group.

VII. What Modernity Removed

Whatever role the varna system played in holding the older structure together, it was substantially dismantled, deliberately and for good reason, by the modern Indian constitutional order, which abolished untouchability and set out, at least formally, to build a state that did not enforce caste hierarchy. That dismantling was a response to real and sustained injustice, and describing its effects honestly means holding two things at once: it addressed genuine harm, and it also removed whatever compulsory, structural glue the older system provided, whether or not that glue was doing anything defensible in the first place.

It’s in this gap, structural cohesion loosened while no replacement institution filled the space, that contemporary anxieties about external religious and cultural influence tend to surface. Part of what feeds this particular anxiety is a matter of public record: groups such as the Joshua Project catalogue and publish detailed profiles of “unreached people groups” by ethnicity, language, and social subgroup, India included, explicitly to direct evangelical outreach. That the data exists and is openly published isn’t in dispute. Where genuine disagreement begins is over how to read the activity itself, as unremarkable outreach of the kind every proselytizing tradition has always practiced, or as something more deliberately targeted at a society’s weakest points, and well-informed people land in very different places on that question. Layered on top is recurring legislative debate over how India regulates foreign funding of religious and charitable organizations: real and long-running, even if its current status is, as of this writing, still unsettled.

VIII. The Missing Political Economy of Dharma

Capitalism and communism, whatever else divides them, share one thing: each is backed by a fully worked-out theory of political economy. Each makes a specific claim about who should own what, how labor and resources should be distributed, and what role the state should play in enforcing that distribution. One can disagree with either theory, but neither is vague about its own mechanics.

There is no real equivalent sitting behind the Hindu political imagination. “Ram Rajya,” invoked by figures from Gandhi onward as shorthand for ideal governance, functions as an aspiration rather than a worked model. It names a mood, virtuous kingship, general welfare, harmony between duty and station, without specifying how property, labor, and status would actually be organized under it, in anything like the exhausting detail a systematic economic theory requires of itself. This may be the more fundamental gap: not a shortage of spiritual writing, of which there is an immense amount, but a shortage of systematic thinking about the political economy a dharmic society would actually run on, stated in terms specific enough to be defended, criticized, or implemented.

IX. Toward the Source

Every thread here eventually leads back to the same recommendation: before any of it can be resolved, it needs checking against the oldest available texts rather than against centuries of accumulated received wisdom about what those texts supposedly say. What does the Vedic corpus actually say about varna, in its own words, rather than in the paraphrase of every intervening commentator? The honest answer is that this remains under-examined, not because the texts are inaccessible, but because doing the work seriously is slow, unglamorous scholarship that competes poorly for attention against louder, more immediate arguments.

This isn’t a project one person finishes. It’s closer to the kind of question that wants a hundred people, each running down a single thread: one on the guild economics of premodern India, one on comparative religious organization, one on the textual history of varna specifically, one on what “Ram Rajya” would need to specify before it counted as an actual theory rather than a slogan. What’s offered here is not a conclusion so much as a map of where the unanswered questions cluster.

A Note on Method

None of the foregoing should be mistaken for settled scholarship. Reading varna primarily as an economic incentive structure is one interpretive lens among several in the study of Indian social history, and it sits uneasily beside caste’s extensively documented role in enabling exclusion and discrimination, the very things India’s constitutional project has spent seventy-five years trying to dismantle. Comparative religion scholars remain divided on “exceptionalism” narratives of Hindu persistence more broadly, and claims about coordinated external efforts to reshape Indian religious demography, however often repeated, are contested and disputed by researchers who study missionary activity and conversion empirically. An essay this compact necessarily compresses real complexity into single sentences that deserve entire books. It’s offered here as a starting map, not a finished argument.
